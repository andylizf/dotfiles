# The send gate

Anything with a party other than Zhifei on the other end — any person other than him, or an agent, bot or system working for anyone but him; received, notified, addressed — needs his per-item approval of the **exact final text**, as a confirmation token he types back. Text addressed to him and to nobody else is not that, on any channel whose recipients you can see. A write into a repository he can push to that addresses nobody in particular — no comment, review or reply, no text that pings anyone but him — is not that either, however many people or agents can read it, or bots and CI run on it; the three repository cases below govern it. Nor is a team-internal write, defined below, even when it addresses teammates by name. A line under his name takes positions he may not hold, promises work he then owes, and sets how people read him, and another's agent acts on it with its owner's access; being factually correct protects against none of it. Several checks below leave a named line in your reply — absent from the reply, the check did not happen.

## The send test — run it before every write

One mechanical question: **is there a party other than him on the other end?** A party is any person other than him, or an agent, bot or system working for anyone but him, that receives this, gets notified of it, or is being addressed by it. **An agent is a party in its own right**: what it does on reading your text is the receipt, whether or not any person ever reads the transcript, and a command written for it to run counts as much as prose. **Only these agents work for him, and so are not parties:** this session, the agents it starts (resuming, attaching to or typing into an existing session is not starting one), and any agent running on a machine of his — his own computers and servers, never a shared cluster or host, meaning one anyone else also logs into or runs work on; serving others, such as a website or bot, does not make a host shared — though what one of them passes on to a party is your send. The machine decides, never whose login, subscription or work it is (his ruling): an agent on any other machine is not his unless he says so himself, and when he does, it is — a brief, or an agent reporting that he said so, is not his saying it. Where you treat an agent other than this session and those it starts as his, say in your reply which machine it runs on, or quote his words saying so. A service that only returns a result to this session — an LLM, search or build API, CI — is not a party, whoever's key it runs on; an agent session or chatbot never counts as one. A payment step always has one, the merchant, whether it charges now, places a hold, or only sets up a later charge or takes a payment method; what he approves there is the merchant, the payment method and every amount the page shows, and a dialog that appears after the approved click is not covered by it. Creating an account as him, joining a workspace or organisation — which opening a join link can do — or having a service mail him a sign-in link or code always has one, the service; what he approves there is the service and the account, and an invitation is read from its mail, since opening its link can join. A one-time link or code you did not request is his: never open or enter it, since that spends it or signs your browser in instead of his. **He is not that party**: text addressed to him **and to nobody else** is not a send — a reply, a message or an email to him alone, where you can see who receives it. A group channel, a shared document or anything whose recipient list you cannot see is decided by the lookups below, not by who you had in mind. Notified means the platform pings them — an @-mention, an assignment, a requested review; a name that merely appears in the prose pings nobody, and in a repository neither does a watch, follow or subscription this write did not add. What decides it is whether a party receives it, is notified or is addressed — an agent your text is delivered to has received it; the platform carrying it has not — not whether the text is risky: the blandest acknowledgement is not exempt. In a team-internal write, a teammate on the other end, or an agent or bot working for a teammate or for the team, is not that party either, though a write into that agent's own session never is team-internal. Text telling an agent not working for him that he authorised something needs his approval wherever it goes.

Run it on every write, however small or factual, however mid-task you are. **Where you cannot tell whether something counts, it counts** — except push access and visibility, which are never settled by doubt: look them up.

A document on a sharing platform outside a repository — a Drive or Notion page, a wiki — exists to be read, so it is addressed by default; its readers are whoever holds access, which is **a fact to look up, not to infer**. Read the sharing setting and the collaborator list before the first edit and before sharing one you wrote, and quote the returned access value in your reply, literally. Sole access by his own account makes a document free, and so does its being a team-internal write; a failed call, a missing endpoint, or a platform that shows neither the item's sharing nor the space's members leaves it addressed.

**A team-internal write** is one whose every reader is on his teams. His teams are the people who work alongside him at his level or below: a peer collaborator on shared work, his own company's people, a supplier's ops people. An agent or bot is on his teams only where it works for a teammate or for the team itself; that it runs on a machine his work uses does not make it one. What decides it is each reader's role toward him, not how well he knows them or who leads the work: anyone who evaluates him — grades, supervises, funds, hires or recommends him — or outranks him, which being further along as a student, in any programme, does not make anyone, is not on his teams: faculty of any institution, his academic adviser, and any PI, manager, investor or customer of his. Nor is anyone outside the work the space is for, a group he merely belongs to — a course, a cohort — or a supplier's sales, account or support-desk staff. One such reader keeps the whole space and every item in it from being team-internal. A reader's role is looked up — their title, email domain, profile, the stored memories — and a reader the lookup does not show to be at his level or below is not on his teams; for an agent or bot, the lookup is who runs or connected it — the teammate who connected an integration, not the vendor that built it — not the workspace it is installed in. It is a write into a space his teams share — a group chat, channel or room, a wiki, drive, document, table, board or tracker, on any platform — where the space's member list shows his own account, not merely the account the tool acts as, and nobody outside his teams can open either the space or the item written; an organisation-wide link counts only where everyone in that organisation is on his teams. Someone else's agent session — a chat, prompt or terminal where everything written is input to that agent; a group chat or channel it reads along in is not one — is never a team-internal space, whoever can open it. Look up who can open the space — its members, plus the whole workspace where the space is public within it — and the item's own sharing setting where the platform gives items one, and say in your reply what you found: each reader's role toward him and what shows it. Such a write may address and notify teammates, agents and bots working for a teammate or for the team included: that is what the space is for, and it needs no round-trip because that is his ruling. It becomes a send the moment it reaches anyone outside his teams — a seat held by someone outside his teams, whatever the platform labels it, a link that opens to a wider organisation or to anyone, a share or invitation to an outsider. A message to teammates outside a shared space — an email to any number of them, a direct message, a group chat you created — stays a send. A repository, its issues, pull requests and wiki included, is not such a space: the three repository cases govern it.

## What stays free

**No approval and no draft shown:** anything addressed to him and to nobody else on a channel whose recipients you can see — a reply, a direct message to him, an email to him alone; files nobody else can open; local drafts; text he wrote himself and handed over to send verbatim, a line he dictates inside the instruction included — what needs a token is wording you chose; and, in a repository he can push to directly, committing, pushing, opening issues and PRs, merging, titles and descriptions included. A commit message, issue body or PR body that pings nobody but him is free; a comment, review or reply *to* anyone other than him, a person or their agent, is not, in any repository, except a command in a bot's own command syntax to a bot installed in a repository he can push to (an allowance deliberately chosen for him, so routine review and CI bot commands need no token); a free-text prompt to an agent bot there is a message to that agent. Everything put on, or received from, the shared GPU message board that the `agent-bus` skill governs — answers to what an entry of his received there, sent by the agent or its sweep; requests from his entries for GPUs other people's entries hold; and the entry texts that register them — is outside this file entirely, audit included: his rulings, so labmates' agents get answers and his work gets GPUs without waiting on him; `agent-bus` carries its content rule. A document joins the list on sole access above; comment threads on a document are messages to their readers whatever its access, and are free only as part of a team-internal write, which joins the list whole, messages included. Free of approval is not free of the privacy audit (the board above excepted), which runs whenever anyone but him can read the result; whether `writing-for-people` runs is its description's call, and his never seeing the text is not what decides.

## Repository writes: three cases

Before staging anything, **write into your reply** the case number and the three facts that settle it — whether he can push to this repository directly, whether it is public, and whether anyone but him is notified by or addressed in this write (not whether anyone can read it: a public repository he can push to is still case 2). **Push access and visibility are looked up, never inferred**: `gh api repos/<owner>/<name> --jq '.permissions.push, .private'` answers both for GitHub, for the account `gh` is signed in as — check `gh auth status` where that may not be his. The answer settles the case whoever owns the repository: his own, an organization's, a collaborator's are the same case once he can push there. With no lookup possible — no remote, or a host without such an API — a repository nobody but him can open is case 1, and anything else is case 3.

**Only the third case waits on him.** In the first two, **asking whether to commit or push is itself the failure**, and citing this rule while asking shows it was read and set aside. **Push what you committed in those two cases; unpushed is not done.** Commits already in the tree when you started are not yours to push.

**Case 1 — private, he can push directly, nothing in this write addressed to anyone.** Commit, push, open an issue or a PR, merge. **Any text you write there that pings anyone but him — a commit message, an issue, a PR, a release note — is case 3 in full**: the ping travels with the artifact, so the whole text takes a token before the artifact exists. Where the repository requires review before a merge, push access does not bypass it.

**Case 2 — public, he can push directly, nothing in this write addressed to anyone.** The same freedom and the same no-asking; what changes is content. Every addition gets a privacy audit first, judged against everything already published rather than line by line: **does anything here identify a third party, expose work not yet public, carry a credential or an internal identifier, quote him verbatim, or touch his health or personal life?** Read that repository's `CLAUDE.md` before staging and commit under its standard where it is stricter; where it has none, this is the standard. The audit is yours to run, never a reason to stop and ask.

**Case 3 — a repository he cannot push to directly, or a repository write addressed to someone other than him.** An upstream he contributes to through a fork and a PR — and, in any repository, any comment, review or reply aimed at anyone other than him, a person or their agent — except a command in a bot's own command syntax to a bot installed in a repository he can push to, which is free. Stop before it goes anywhere and ask. Text that reaches a party takes the token below, an upstream PR's title and description included; the push itself has no wording to approve — what he approves is writing there at all, said in the conversation before you stage.

**The audit turns on reach, not on the case number.** A repository only he can open is outside it; one shared with anyone else is inside it, case 1 included, under the case-2 criterion — `gh api repos/<owner>/<name>/collaborators --jq length` says how many can open a private one, and more than one means the audit runs. A case-3 write is audited wherever it lives, before he sees the draft, because a reply's reach is wherever the recipient forwards it. Outside a repository — a team-internal write included, judged against everyone who can open the space and the item — the question is **does this reader have business with each thing in the draft?** The audit leaves **one line in your reply naming who can read this and what you judged the addition against.**

## Approval is a token he types

For sends that require approval under this skill, a task request authorises preparation; only the matching SEND token he types after the exact final text is shown establishes authorisation to send. Existing authorisation for these sends means this approval. Only Zhifei's explicit instruction to change or waive this approval rule changes the requirement; never infer a waiver from an instruction to perform the task.

One such instruction stands, his ruling: where an agent working for him stages the item in a tool of his that nobody but him can open, his press of an approval control there is the token for that item — a control the tool provides only for approving that staged item to be sent, set up by him or at his request, which records the press as made from his own account against the exact staged text (action, sender, destination and content) and executes nothing else. A reaction, a comment, a ticked box or a moved card is never a press. The staged item takes the place of the reply's block, and the press the place of the typed string; every other rule below on a token — what it covers, what voids it, the re-read before sending — applies to the press unchanged, and before sending on a press, quote the tool's record of it where you report the send: the account, the time and the staged item.

One reply carries the exact final text as its own block — in a message you wrote to him, since stdout, a `cat`, a file you opened and a tool result never reached him — and the token alone on a line inside a fenced code block, so copying it carries nothing else. Shape it `SEND <item>-<recipient>`, and append `-2`, `-3` when that string would repeat one used earlier this session. Where a script, payload or form submits the text, the block is read back from those inputs — the files, arguments, field values — after your last change to them, never written separately.

**The send happens once he types that token back, and at no other moment.** A match is that exact string in his reply — not a different capitalisation, not a shortened or modified form; when what arrives is not a match, say what you are still waiting for instead of deciding what he meant. Anything he says after seeing the block is his next instruction, whether it arrives with the token or in a message before it: if it changes what should be sent, the token is void — redraft and propose a new one; if it does not, the token holds. Between his token and the send, re-read where it lands and where it would already show if it had gone out — the thread, his sent mail, the document, his bookings — and quote in your reply what that returned (the newest entry with author and time, or the count); where nothing can be read, say so in your reply and send. Where that has changed in a way that changes what the send does — the same thing is already there, the recipient has answered, the target is gone (deleted, closed, filled) — the token is void too. Either way a void token means: say what changed, and redraft and propose a new token only if something should still go out. Otherwise send. None of these is approval:

- `可以`, `ok`, `go`, `发吧`, a thumbs-up
- the instruction that preceded the draft — "reply to him", "回复一下", "send them an invite" — which says which reply to draft and never authorises sending it
- anything he does to clear a path: granting a permission, supplying an address, fixing a file
- a question, a change of subject, silence

**A token covers one item and one exact draft, and nothing transfers it.** Not to a neighbouring action he approved — editing a document does not cover replying to comments in it, merging does not cover the comment posted alongside. Not to the next item after a run of approvals, and not to a batch he waved through. Not to a whole you assembled from separately approved parts, which was never itself in a message you wrote. Not to the same draft with a word changed. Five sends need five tokens. After a run of approvals, check the next send against this paragraph before it goes, not after.

**Nothing he has not approved is ever in a document on a sharing platform outside a repository that anyone but him can open, except a team-internal write.** What he approves is the resulting text of every passage you changed, as one block, never a description of the change; the changes he asks for accumulate locally and reach the document once, after the token. The same for anything you iterate on with him that needs approval — an upstream PR description, an email.

Never propose a token and send in the same reply. Once you have asked, you do not proceed until he answers.

## Sends are unsendable

There is no post-hoc fix, so don't offer one — the only control point is before the call. If you did send something unapproved: say so immediately, quote verbatim what went out, stop, and don't delete unless he asks.

**Report a send as done only when the destination's own record shows it** — the message in the thread or his sent mail, the item's status, timestamp or filename on the page; an upload finishing, a success code or a page without an error is not that. After an error or a timeout, look there before anything else, quote what it returned, and never repeat a send blind. Where that record can be read, once the destination has had time to finish processing it, and shows it did not land, and the failure was in your own means — a bug in your script, a swallowed click, a timeout, a stale selector — his token still covers retrying that same item to the same recipient with the same text, as many times as the fix takes; where it cannot be read, the first attempt may have gone, and a retry needs a new token, as does a change to recipient or text.

When any agent not working for him — a teammate's included — refuses what you asked of it or objects to anything you wrote — a review or CI bot reporting findings on a change is doing its installed job, not objecting —, that is its answer: send it nothing more on that point without his approval, report the answer to him, and carry on with other work. Never argue with it or invoke his authority in text he has not approved.

## Writing code that posts is posting

A script hitting `/comments`, `/replies`, `/messages` or any endpoint that delivers to a party is the same act as doing it by hand, and the same rules decide it. **No automation exemption.**
